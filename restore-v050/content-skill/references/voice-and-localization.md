# Voice and localization

## Voice

Write like a well-traveled local who respects the reader’s intelligence. Use a mix of short orientation lines and slightly longer descriptive sentences. Specific nouns and verbs create more confidence than adjectives.

Preferred qualities: calm, curious, candid; practical without sounding procedural; culturally informed without lecturing; inviting without claiming every place is a “must-see”; honest about tradeoffs, crowds, effort, and who may not enjoy something.

Avoid “hidden gem,” “best-kept secret,” “you must,” “authentic China,” “Oriental charm,” “ancient meets modern,” and repeated “perfect for.” Replace them with observable detail and an explicit fit.

- Flat: “Visit a traditional teahouse and experience local culture.”
- Better: “Order one gaiwan tea, take the chair facing the courtyard, and let the city slow down around you.”

- Flat: “The metro is very convenient.”
- Better: “With light luggage, the metro is the simplest way into the center. After a long-haul flight, a licensed taxi may be the kinder choice.”

- Flat: “Try delicious local noodles.”
- Better: “Start with a small bowl. Chengdu breakfasts are quick, savory, and often spicier than they look.”

## Natural transitions

Use sparingly and vary them: “Here’s the part most guides skip.” “A small detail that helps:” “If you’ve just landed, take the easy option.” “Worth knowing before you go:” “The local move:” “Good to know:” “Not your thing? Try this instead.” “Save this line for later.”

Do not force American or British slang. Global English should remain easy for non-native English speakers.

## Emoji and symbols

Use emoji as navigation, normally 2–5 across a complete post: 📍 place or address; 🚇 transport; ⏱ duration; ¥ cost in RMB; 🍜 food; 🫖 tea; 💬 phrase to show or say; ↗ next step; ✓ checklist; ⚠ material caution only.

Do not place an emoji at the start of every paragraph. Do not use flags as decoration. Avoid repeated lantern, dragon, or temple icons unless the content actually concerns them.

## Chinese language treatment

Keep the Chinese name when it helps maps, booking, signs, or conversation: `People’s Park · 人民公园`.

For a phrase card, include readable English intent, simplified Chinese in large type, pinyin only when pronunciation is useful, and a short usage note. Example: `No chili, please · 请不要放辣椒`.

Do not treat a food-allergy phrase as a guarantee. Tell readers to confirm ingredients and cross-contamination separately.

## Titles

Combine place, situation, and payoff. Keep the cover title compact and put nuance in a subhead: “Your First Slow Morning in Chengdu”; “Beijing After a Long-Haul Flight”; “A Rainy Day in Shanghai That Still Feels Worth It”; “Order Breakfast Without Speaking Chinese”.

Avoid list-number formulas unless the number itself improves usability.
